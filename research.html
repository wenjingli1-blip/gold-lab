Project I

Transposable elements: address codes for lncRNAs?

Computational Experimental

The sequence domains underlying long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) activities remain largely unknown. We have proposed that these domains can originate from neofunctionalised fragments of transposable elements (TEs), otherwise known as RIDLs — Repeat Insertion Domains of Long Noncoding RNA. A small but growing number of RIDLs have been identified.

We are interested in identifying RIDLs and understanding how they contribute to lncRNAs' biological activity. An important challenge is to discern true RIDLs against a far more numerous background of neutrally-evolving, non-functional TEs. We have recently found evidence that a subset of TE types experience evolutionary selection in the context of lncRNA exons, and that their host lncRNAs tend to be functionally validated and associated with disease.

An emerging question in the lncRNA field is how specific subcellular locations of lncRNAs are encoded in their primary sequence. We hypothesised that RIDLs may play a role in directing lncRNAs to subcellular compartments. We use global localisation data from human cell lines to identify a role for evolutionarily-conserved L2b, MIRb and MIRc elements in regulating nuclear/cytoplasmic distribution of lncRNAs.

Key References

The RIDL hypothesis — Johnson and Guigó (2014)

TEs and Localisation — Carlevaro-Fita et al. (2019)

Project II

LncRNAs that drive cancer

Cancer Computational

Tumours develop through the acquisition of driver mutations that enable cells to replicate uncontrollably and invade other sites. Genes containing driver mutations are attractive therapeutic targets, but it remains unknown whether these can include long noncoding RNAs.

As part of our work in the International Cancer Genome Consortium's PCAWG (PanCancer Analysis of Whole Genomes) project, we have developed ExInAtor, a bioinformatic pipeline to identify driver lncRNAs using somatic mutations from whole tumour genomes. ExInAtor compares the observed mutation rate in lncRNA exons against a background model to identify candidate driver lncRNAs with statistical confidence.

This work positions lncRNAs as a new, largely unexplored class of cancer driver genes — with direct implications for precision oncology target selection and therapeutic development.

Key Reference

ExInAtor — Lanzós A. et al, Scientific Reports (2017)

ExInAtor Available via GOLD Lab tools

Project III

CRISPR-Cas9 tools for lncRNAs

Tools Experimental

CRISPR-Cas9 is a powerful and versatile genome-editing tool that has revolutionised the field of long noncoding RNA research. We have developed a suite of tools for single-gene and high-throughput functional analysis of lncRNAs.

DECKO

Dual Excision CRISPR Knockout

A simple and scalable vector system that delivers paired sgRNAs for deletion of lncRNAs or other genomic elements. Scalable from single-gene to genome-wide experiments.

CRISPETa

CRISPR Paired Excision Tool

A bioinformatic design tool for paired sgRNAs, accessible to non-specialists and bioinformaticians alike. Supports both single-gene and genome-wide deletion experiments.

CASPR

CRISPR Analysis for Single and Paired RNA-guides

Our most recent tool: an end-to-end analysis pipeline for CRISPR screen analysis, enabling systematic functional interrogation of the lncRNA genome.

Key References

DECKO — Aparicio-Prat E. et al, BMC Genomics (2015)

CRISPETa — Pulido-Quetglas C. et al, PLOS Computational Biology (2017)

Project IV

LncRNA & Heart Regeneration

Therapeutic Experimental

Heart regeneration is a biological process of utmost importance to medicine. It is known that lncRNAs play critical roles in the molecular networks mediating the response of cardiac cells to damage, such as that caused by myocardial infarction. We use a variety of strategies to identify lncRNAs involved in this process.

This project is part of an international collaborative effort combining experimental cardiac biology with our lncRNA discovery and functional analysis expertise, aiming to identify novel RNA-based therapeutic targets for heart disease.

Collaborators

Project V

LncRNA Characterisation

Computational Experimental

To date only 2% of lncRNAs has been functionally characterised, and little is known about their molecular mechanisms and transcript properties. Bioinformatic tools applied to the protein-coding world are generally ineffective for lncRNAs, complicating their classification and prediction of functions.

A major bottleneck is the lack of a framework for categorising lncRNAs and understanding how molecular functions are encoded in their sequence. In this context, we are especially focused on characterisation through the study of their subcellular localisation and embedded repeat elements.

We leverage global localisation datasets from human cell lines to map the determinants of nuclear versus cytoplasmic lncRNA distribution, identifying sequence signatures — including RIDL elements — that encode subcellular addresses.

Key References

The RIDL hypothesis — Johnson and Guigó (2014)

Repeats and subcellular localisation — Carlevaro-Fita et al. (2019)

Project VI

LncRNA & Diagnostics

Therapeutic Experimental

We are excited about the possibility of developing rapid and cheap diagnostics for early-stage diseases based on RNA profiles. The RNA content, or transcriptome, of an organ presents a dynamic and data-rich snapshot of its physiological state. RNA sequencing therefore affords the possibility of rapid and cheap diagnostics of early-stage disease.

In a collaborative project funded by La Marató de TV3, we are developing new RNA biomarkers in cardiac disease — with the goal of enabling earlier, non-invasive diagnosis of cardiac insufficiency through lncRNA expression signatures.

Collaborator

Josep Comin-Colet — Director, Cardiac Insufficiency Program, Hospital del Mar, Barcelona

Funded by La Marató de TV3

Project VII

GENCODE Annotation

Computational

Genomics depends on the availability of high-quality annotations — curated collections of genes. The worldwide reference annotation of lncRNAs in human and mouse is managed by GENCODE, a collaboration led by the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute and funded by the National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI).

Since 2010, Rory Johnson has contributed to this project, through the first landmark paper (Derrien, Johnson et al.) and more recently through the development of Capture Long Read Sequencing (CLS) to improve and expand lncRNA annotations.

CLS applies targeted capture to long-read sequencing platforms, dramatically improving the completeness and accuracy of lncRNA transcript models in the GENCODE reference — a resource used by researchers worldwide.

Key References

GENCODE lncRNA annotation — Derrien, Johnson et al.

Capture Long Read Sequencing — Lagarde et al.
